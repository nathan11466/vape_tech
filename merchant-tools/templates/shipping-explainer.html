Short version: the US Postal Service is not allowed to deliver vapes to your door, the big private couriers stopped carrying them too, and the shop you buy from has to verify your age against a government records database before it accepts the order and get an adult signature when it arrives. None of that is the shop being awkward. It is federal law, and it applies to every online vape seller in the country.

This page explains what the rules actually say, what they mean for an order you place today, and which parts are decided by your state instead.

The law behind it

The Prevent All Cigarette Trafficking Act — the PACT Act, at 15 U.S.C. §§ 375–378 — has governed mail-order cigarettes since 2010. In December 2020 Congress extended it to vapes, by the straightforward device of writing them into the definition of "cigarette": § 375(2)(A)(ii)(II) now includes "an electronic nicotine delivery system".

That definition is broader than most people expect. Under § 375(7) it covers e-cigarettes, e-hookahs, e-cigars, vape pens, refillable personal vaporisers and electronic pipes — and also "any component, liquid, part, or accessory" of one, whether or not it is sold separately from the device. A replacement coil on its own is covered. So is an empty tank. The only carve-out is for products the FDA has approved as cessation aids and which are sold solely for that purpose.

Why it cannot come through the Post Office

Since 21 October 2021, under the Postal Service's Treatment of E-Cigarettes in the Mail rule, vapes are nonmailable. There are three narrow exceptions in Publication 52, section 473, and a consumer order is not one of them:

ExceptionWho it is forDoes it cover a normal online order?
473.2 — intra-Alaska and intra-Hawaii Shipments that start and finish in the same non-contiguous state, handed over face to face at a counter No — only within Alaska or within Hawaii
473.3 — business and regulatory Licensed businesses and government agencies, business-to-business only, on an approved application No — a consumer cannot be the recipient
473.4 — certain individuals An adult sending a small non-commercial package: a gift, a return, a recycling shipment. Ten mailings per 30 days, ten ounces, in person with photo ID No — not a sale

The other two exceptions on that page, for consumer testing and public health research, apply to cigarettes only and expressly exclude vapes. There is no exception at all for international mail or for APO, FPO and DPO addresses — so a vape cannot be posted overseas or to a service address, however it was bought.

This is also why a shop will refuse a PO Box. A PO Box is a Postal Service address, and the Postal Service cannot bring the parcel.

Why the big couriers will not carry it either

The PACT Act does not ban private carriers from handling vapes, but it makes them part of the enforcement machinery. Under § 376a(e) a carrier must not knowingly complete a delivery for a seller on the federal non-compliant list, and the statute specifically protects carriers from being penalised for refusing or delaying such deliveries.

Faced with that, the large national carriers stopped accepting vapour products for consumer delivery in 2021 — a position still reported in 2025. Carrier policy is set by the carrier rather than by statute, so it can change and is worth confirming with them directly, but the practical position has been stable for years.

What is left is regional and specialist couriers. That is the reason vape delivery is slower than you are used to, costs more than a comparable parcel of anything else, and sometimes is not available to your area at all.

What the shop has to do before your order ships

These are obligations on the seller, not on you, but they explain every unusual thing checkout asks for. From 15 U.S.C. § 376a:

  • Verify who you are before accepting the order. The seller must obtain your full name, date of birth and residential address, and check them against a commercially available database built mainly from government records — one it does not own, control or get to edit (§ 376a(b)(4)(A)(iii), (b)(4)(B)). This is why a nickname or an old address fails, and why a shop cannot simply take your word for it.
  • Obey your state's law as if the sale happened there. Section 376a(a)(3) requires the seller to follow the destination's excise taxes, licensing, stamping and sales restrictions "as if the delivery sales occurred entirely within the specific State and place". A state-level rule is therefore the seller's problem too, not only yours.
  • Pay the state and local excise tax up front. Before the parcel is even handed to a carrier, the tax must be paid and any required stamps affixed (§ 376a(d)(1)). In a high-tax state that is a large part of why the same device costs more delivered to you than to someone three states away.
  • Register and report. A seller shipping into a taxing state must register with the ATF and file monthly reports with that state's tobacco tax administrator, due on the 10th (§ 376). The ATF has confirmed these duties apply whichever carrier is used.
  • Keep the records for years. Each delivery sale must be recorded and kept until the end of the fourth full calendar year after it (§ 376a(c)(2)), available to state tax administrators and attorneys general.

The penalties are real rather than nominal. A knowing violation carries up to three years' imprisonment (§ 377(a)(1)), and the civil penalty for a delivery seller is the greater of $5,000 for a first offence — $10,000 after that — or 2% of a year's gross sales. For a mid-sized retailer that second figure is the one that ends the business. When a shop refuses your state, this is usually what it is refusing.

What to expect when you order

  • An age check at checkout that can fail. It runs against records, so the name and address have to be the ones on file for you.
  • Somebody over 21 must be home. The shipping method has to require a signature at the delivery address from you or another adult of legal age, who shows a valid government-issued photo ID (§ 376a(b)(4)(A)(ii)). It cannot be left on the step. A neighbour of 21 or over can sign for it; a sixteen-year-old cannot.
  • 21 everywhere. The federal minimum age for buying any tobacco product, vapes included, has been 21 since 20 December 2019 (FDA, Tobacco 21). A handful of states still have 18 or 19 printed in their own statutes. Those are superseded.
  • No PO Box, no APO or FPO address, no international.
  • Ten pounds per order. A single sale or delivery may not exceed ten pounds (§ 376a(b)(3)), which a bulk e-liquid order can genuinely reach.
  • Slower, and dearer. Specialist couriers, adult signature service and pre-paid state excise tax all land in the shipping line.

Where your state comes in

Everything above is federal and applies identically in all fifty states. On top of it, each state sets its own rules on tax, licensing, flavours and whether a vape may be posted to a consumer there at all — and on that last question a few states say no. Our state pages carry a sourced note on the local position for each one, with the statute cited.

It is worth reading before you order: a shop's shipping page tells you where that shop is willing to post, which is not the same question as where a sale is permitted.

What this page does not cover

Hemp-derived products — delta-8, THC-A, CBD — sit under a different and faster-moving set of rules, and the PACT Act's reach over them has been litigated rather than settled. Several states have also brought in product registry laws since 2025, requiring manufacturers to certify a product before it may be sold there, which changes what a shop may lawfully send you even where shipping itself is fine. Neither is covered here.
